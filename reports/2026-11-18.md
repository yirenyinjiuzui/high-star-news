## Where to start today

Today follows a local media workflow: record a demonstration with OBS Studio, edit speech with Audacity, extract useful video segments with LosslessCut, inspect or convert files with FFmpeg, and review playback with mpv. Start with the one task you already have; these are alternatives for different stages, not a required five-tool installation.

## 1. OBS Studio

Compose a screen, camera and microphone into a reusable recording scene.

### What it does

OBS captures sources, combines them in scenes, encodes video and records locally or streams. A scene can include a window capture, webcam and text, with audio devices selected separately. The official quick-start guide covers the configuration wizard, sources, meters and test recordings: https://obsproject.com/kb/quick-start-guide .

### Why we recommend it

Recommend it when a repeatable demonstration needs more control than a single screen-record button. Saving a scene makes the composition explicit; recording quality still depends on device setup and encoder choices.

### How it could help you

1. Record a software walkthrough with one application window and spoken explanation, then check that menus and pointer actions remain readable.
2. Prepare a practice presentation with slides and a small camera view, and review microphone balance before making the final recording.

### Who it fits and what to expect

Fits Windows, macOS and Linux users who can configure sources and permissions; less suitable for someone wanting only a one-click clip or a video editor. OBS itself is free under GPL-2.0-or-later; third-party plugins and streaming services have separate terms. Encoding consumes CPU/GPU and storage; streaming also needs upload bandwidth. Capture methods differ by platform, and a powerful computer does not guarantee a suitable recording configuration.

### A small first step

Allow 25 minutes: use the auto-configuration wizard for recording, add one window and microphone, make a one-minute local sample, then watch it for legibility, silence and missing audio. Change one setting and compare. Keep this trial local; streaming is unnecessary.

## 2. Audacity

Record speech and arrange audio on a visible multitrack timeline.

### What it does

Audacity is a desktop multitrack audio editor and recorder. It can capture a microphone and let you edit recorded material in tracks. The official microphone guide explains device selection, mono recording and checking clipping by listening and watching levels: https://support.audacityteam.org/basics/recording-your-voice-and-microphone .

### Why we recommend it

Recommend it for deliberate work on speech recordings when you want to see and review edits. It separates audio preparation from video capture; it cannot repair every problem caused by a poor microphone or distorted input.

### How it could help you

1. Record a short spoken explanation, remove an unwanted opening section and listen to the transition before exporting a copy.
2. Place two practice narration takes in separate tracks, compare delivery and keep the clearer take for a presentation.

### Who it fits and what to expect

Fits Windows, macOS and GNU/Linux users editing local audio; not a substitute for video editing or automatic transcription. The application is free and GPLv3, with per-file and third-party exceptions described in LICENSE.txt. A microphone is needed for new recordings, and long multitrack sessions require disk space. The README says master is undergoing major Audacity 4 UI and structural changes, while 3.x work uses audacity3; choose a published release rather than assuming master instructions match it. External plugins or services may have separate prices and terms.

### A small first step

Spend 20–25 minutes with a published release: choose the correct microphone, make a 30-second mono sample, listen for clipping, trim a copy and export it to a new file. Reopen the exported file and compare with the original. Menu names can differ by release.

## 3. LosslessCut

Extract useful sections and rearrange tracks without routinely re-encoding everything.

### What it does

This FFmpeg-powered desktop app trims media, merges compatible segments, extracts or replaces tracks, edits metadata and remuxes containers. Its README distinguishes preview playback from exporting the original streams. Official limitations: https://losslesscut.app/docs/troubleshooting .

### Why we recommend it

Recommend it for rough selection from existing recordings when preserving encoded media matters more than adding effects. Copying streams avoids an additional encode in supported operations, but does not imply frame-accurate cuts or universal compatibility.

### How it could help you

1. Keep the useful demonstration section from a long screen recording and inspect both ends of the exported segment.
2. Extract an audio track from a recording, or remove an unwanted track from a copy before sharing the result.

### Who it fits and what to expect

Fits desktop Windows, macOS and Linux users doing rough cuts and track operations; not ideal for transitions, titles or guaranteed arbitrary-frame cuts. Manual official downloads are free; paid store versions support development and offer store installation/updates. The repository is GPL-2.0; bundled components have their own licensing. Expect space for input and exported media. Preview codec support varies, keyframes constrain cuts, and merging needs compatible streams. Test exported playback and audio sync; do not delete originals based solely on a successful export message.

### A small first step

In 20 minutes, open a short disposable copy, mark one 15-second segment and export to a new file. Play its start and end in a separate player and compare the requested boundaries and audio sync. If the boundary shifts, read the keyframe-cut section rather than assuming data corruption.

## 4. FFmpeg

Inspect streams and perform repeatable audio/video transformations.

### What it does

The toolkit includes ffmpeg for conversion and streaming, ffprobe for stream inspection and ffplay for simple playback, alongside codec, container and filtering libraries. The official site documents commands at https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg.html and identifies this GitHub repository as a mirror at https://ffmpeg.org/download.html .

### Why we recommend it

Recommend it when a visible command and repeatable settings matter: first inspect the file, then decide whether remuxing, stream copying or re-encoding is actually needed. This control comes with a steeper learning curve than a visual editor.

### How it could help you

1. Inspect a recording's video and audio streams before investigating why it fails to play in another application.
2. Apply the same conversion settings to a small set of presentation clips after validating one representative output.

### Who it fits and what to expect

Fits command-line users on Linux, Windows and macOS; unsuitable for someone expecting a timeline editor or one universally correct conversion preset. Upstream supplies source and links external binary providers; build features and codec availability differ. Most code is LGPL with optional GPL components; additional build options and dependencies can affect redistribution terms. Source is available without a purchase, but packaging, commercial integration and codec rights require separate consideration. Re-encoding consumes CPU/GPU, time and storage and can reduce quality; stream copy cannot perform arbitrary filtered edits.

### A small first step

Allow 20–30 minutes using a build linked from the official download page. Run ffprobe sample.mp4 on a non-sensitive copy, identify its streams, then try ffmpeg -i sample.mp4 -vn sample.wav to create a separate audio file. Check duration and playback. This example requires a usable input audio stream and is not a universal conversion recipe.

## 5. mpv

Review local media with a minimal player and configurable playback behavior.

### What it does

mpv is a command-line media player supporting many media containers, codecs and subtitle types. It provides a minimal playback interface rather than a full library manager. The README links the manual and installation options: https://mpv.io/manual/master/ and https://mpv.io/installation/ .

### Why we recommend it

Recommend it as a separate playback check after recording or exporting, particularly if you prefer keyboard control and explicit configuration. Successful playback in mpv alone does not establish compatibility with every recipient's device.

### How it could help you

1. Review an exported presentation clip independently from the editor to spot missing audio or an unexpected start point.
2. Open a local video with its subtitle file and assess legibility and timing before distributing study material.

### Who it fits and what to expect

Fits users comfortable with a minimal interface on current Linux, Windows 10 1607+ or macOS 10.15+ per README; not a rich media-library or editing suite. Software is free, GPLv2-or-later by default, with LGPLv2.1-or-later available through a restricted build configuration. The installation page lists semi-official and third-party packages, whose packaging varies. CPU/GPU capability matters for high-resolution media; hardware decoding is not enabled by default and is selected with --hwdec. Old hardware and unsupported OS versions are not guaranteed to work.

### A small first step

Spend 15–20 minutes with a package from the installation page: run mpv sample.mp4, practice pause and seek, then load a local subtitle and check its timing. Compare the same clip in its intended destination player. Leave scripts and extensive configuration for later.
